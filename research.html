The global carbon cycle

Rising atmospheric CO2 from fossil fuel emissions is the principal driver of global warming since the industrial era. About half of these emissions are taken up by the land biosphere and the ocean, which slows the accumulation of CO2 in the atmosphere and moderates the warming it would otherwise cause. These natural sinks respond to rising CO2 and to climate variability, yet their magnitude and evolution remain highly uncertain. Precise estimates of the sinks are therefore needed to project future climate change.

My research involves constraining the global carbon cycle using atmospheric measurements of O2 and CO2. Comparing the results with models that simulate ocean and land processes reveals systematic biases in those models and provides an observational constraint on the global carbon budget.

Current research involves building a multi-tracer Bayesian inversion of the global carbon cycle that constrains the annual net land and ocean carbon sinks consistently with both the atmospheric and the ocean-interior observation records and with the physical couplings between air-sea fluxes of heat, O2, and CO2.

Southern Ocean carbon and oxygen dynamics

The Southern Ocean is the largest ocean carbon reservoir, yet the magnitude and variability of its carbon sink remain highly uncertain. Observations there are sparse, and models carry structural errors, so their estimates of Southern Ocean carbon uptake largely disagree with observations.

I use airborne observations to constrain basin-scale fluxes from the atmosphere. Airborne measurements of O2 alongside CO2 separate the biological and physical processes that drive the fluxes and provide mechanistic insight into model errors. The observations indicate stronger seasonal carbon uptake and biological productivity than process based models simulate.

Current research improves the method, refining how airborne observations and atmospheric mixing timescale are combined so that the flux estimates become more precise and less sensitive to transport error. My research also supports ongoing airborne campaigns to extend the constraint from single seasons to interannual and decadal timescales.

Isentropic coordinates for tracer transport

Comparing sparse aircraft profiles to models is hard because latitude–pressure space mixes air masses that the atmosphere itself keeps separate. I developed a mass-weighted isentropic coordinate that reorders the atmosphere by potential temperature and mass, so that tracer distributions and inventories become far less sensitive to sampling gaps.

Airborne observations and transport model evaluation

Transport error is one of the largest and least examined uncertainties in atmospheric inverse estimates of surface fluxes. Working with data from ATom, ORCAS, HIPPO, and related campaigns, I evaluate how well global models move tracers, and quantify how much of the spread in published flux estimates is transport rather than surface signal.
