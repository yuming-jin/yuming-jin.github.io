Research

A common thread runs through my work: the atmosphere integrates surface fluxes over large scales, so if we can measure it precisely enough and transport it faithfully enough, it becomes a global-scale flux instrument.

Southern Ocean carbon uptake and biological productivity

The Southern Ocean dominates the global ocean carbon sink, yet estimates from surface pCO2 data, float observations, and models disagree sharply on the seasonal cycle of uptake. Using airborne and surface atmospheric observations, I derived an independent constraint on Southern Ocean CO2 exchange, and subsequently on the biological productivity that drives it — finding productivity and carbon uptake stronger than several process-based models suggest.

Atmospheric oxygen as a constraint on air–sea exchange

Atmospheric potential oxygen (APO) — a tracer combining O2 and CO2 that is nearly insensitive to land biosphere exchange — isolates the ocean’s signature in the air. I use APO from airborne campaigns and flask networks to quantify air–sea O2 and CO2 exchange, and to test whether ocean biogeochemistry models reproduce the seasonal and latitudinal structure the atmosphere actually sees.

Isentropic coordinates for tracer transport

Comparing sparse aircraft profiles to models is hard because latitude–pressure space mixes air masses that the atmosphere itself keeps separate. I developed a mass-weighted isentropic coordinate that reorders the atmosphere by potential temperature and mass, so that tracer distributions and inventories become far less sensitive to sampling gaps.

Airborne observations and transport model evaluation

Transport error is one of the largest and least examined uncertainties in atmospheric inverse estimates of surface fluxes. Working with data from ATom, ORCAS, HIPPO, and related campaigns, I evaluate how well global models move tracers, and quantify how much of the spread in published flux estimates is transport rather than surface signal.
