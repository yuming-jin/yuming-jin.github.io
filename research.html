Research

A common thread runs through my work: the atmosphere integrates surface fluxes over large scales, so if we can measure it precisely enough and transport it faithfully enough, it becomes a global-scale flux instrument. Most of what follows is about earning both of those “enough”s.

Atmospheric oxygen as a constraint on the ocean carbon sink

Atmospheric potential oxygen (APO) — a tracer combining O2 and CO2 that is nearly insensitive to land biosphere exchange — isolates the ocean’s signature in the air. I use APO from airborne campaigns and flask networks to quantify air–sea O2 and CO2 exchange, and to test whether ocean biogeochemistry models reproduce the seasonal and latitudinal structure that the atmosphere actually sees. Recent work extends this to a machine learning–based global gridded air–sea O2 flux product, and to the coupling between ocean heat, oxygen, and carbon that governs the climate-driven carbon sink.

Southern Ocean carbon and biological productivity

The Southern Ocean dominates the global ocean carbon sink, yet estimates from surface pCO2 data, float observations, and models have disagreed sharply on the seasonal cycle of uptake. Using airborne and surface atmospheric observations I derived an independent constraint on Southern Ocean CO2 exchange, and subsequently on the biological productivity that drives it — finding productivity and carbon uptake stronger than several process-based models suggest.

Isentropic coordinates for tracer transport

Comparing sparse aircraft profiles to models is hard because latitude–pressure space mixes air masses that the atmosphere itself keeps separate. I developed a mass-weighted isentropic coordinate that reorders the atmosphere by potential temperature and mass, so that tracer distributions and inventories become far less sensitive to sampling gaps. The framework has since been used to diagnose diabatic mixing, to evaluate transport in inversion systems, and to compute atmospheric carbon inventories from campaign data.

Airborne observations and transport model evaluation

Transport error is one of the largest and least examined uncertainties in atmospheric inverse estimates of surface fluxes. Working with data from ATom, ORCAS, HIPPO, and related campaigns, I evaluate how well global models move tracers — and, through community efforts such as the Atmospheric Potential Oxygen forward Model Intercomparison Project (APO-MIP) and TransCom, quantify how much of the spread in published flux estimates is transport rather than surface signal.
